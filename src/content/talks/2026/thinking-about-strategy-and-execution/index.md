*In March 2026, I gave a talk to a group of product managers, senior product managers and product leaders on honing their thoughts on strategy and execution, using books I've found useful in day-to-day work. I shared the physical copies I had with anyone who was interested.*

## Creating vs. compounding

**[Zero to One](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0804139296), Peter Thiel:** Real value comes from creating something genuinely new rather than competing to copy what already works. Most useful when you're building a new product or category and need to find what you know that others don't.

**[7 Powers](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0998116319), Hamilton Helmer:** A framework for durable advantage, where each of seven "powers" (from scale economies to switching costs) pairs a benefit with a barrier competitors can't easily cross. Most useful in an established business deciding which advantages to build and protect.

The two aren't mutually exclusive, but they start from different places: Thiel is about creating something new, Helmer about keeping an advantage you already have.

## Learning good strategy from bad

**[Good Strategy Bad Strategy](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0307886239), Richard Rumelt:** Good strategy rests on three parts: a clear diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy for meeting it, and coherent actions that carry it out. Bad strategy is recognizable too: fluff, failing to face the real challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and objectives that don't address the problem.

## Leading through adversity

**[The Hard Thing About Hard Things](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0062273205), Ben Horowitz:** A candid account of leading through crises where no option is good, from layoffs to near-failure. Its lesson is that resilience and deciding under uncertainty are core parts of the job, not exceptions to it.

## Managing for execution

**[The Goal](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0884271951), Eliyahu M. Goldratt:** A business novel that introduces the theory of constraints: a system's output is limited by its bottleneck, so improving anything else is wasted effort. For product teams, find the real constraint in how work flows before optimizing everything.

**[High Output Management](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0679762884), Andy Grove:** A manager's output is the output of their organization, so the job is choosing the highest-leverage activities. Grove uses everyday examples like running a breakfast kitchen to explain production trade-offs and how to structure an organization.
