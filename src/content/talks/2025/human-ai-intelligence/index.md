*In November 2025, I gave this talk to the Seattle chapter of TPM Events, a community for technical program managers (TPMs) that takes part in global events like TPM Basecamp. The recording and slides are at the end of this page.*

Human intelligence, to me, is your ability to work with others. You can't afford to leave it behind while chasing artificial intelligence, but it's not an either-or: you can use AI to get better at it.

It matters most for TPMs, whose job is aligning priorities. Goals get distilled into smaller team goals that are measured locally, so every team protects its own. Aligning humans is where great TPMs distinguish themselves from good ones.

## Four things you can do right now

1. **Meet lots of humans:** Try to meet one new human you haven't met before every week. Many people don't because it feels like a transaction. It's not. It's your curiosity in knowing what they're about. Even if you skip a week, that's 30 new people a year, and over 10 years, a beautiful, healthy network.
2. **Ask about goals:** Don't ask, "Let's talk about this project." Ask what they're trying to achieve. You can ask your CEO, your VP, or your manager. It structures the conversation in a forward-looking way, and it's where you start stitching local goals back into the bigger goals they were distilled from.
3. **Align goals:** A 2-fer is when you achieve two goals with one amount of effort. Great leaders align individual, team, and business unit goals; good leaders align at least two. When I took on my organization a few years ago, a tenet became that we only pick up 2-fers or 3-fers. A simple example: retire the legacy service lurking in the corner. You gain margins, and the dev team is happier because they didn't want to maintain it anyway.
4. **Segment your work:** Have a mental map of where you spend your time: gathering inputs, writing reports, filing tickets. For every segment that doesn't involve a human, think about how you'd write an agent for it over time. The segment where you meet with humans, don't let go of that. Maximize it.

## Using AI to feed your human intelligence

5. **Use AI to construct your arguments:** TPMs are in a lot of debates: this should be prioritized, that should be prioritized. Use AI to construct your argument so it lands a punch. If questions don't come to you naturally on the fly, ask AI for the three questions you could ask. Picking the right words is sometimes half the battle.
6. **Use AI as a conversational assistant:** I've learned a lot of topics driving to work, from stablecoins to private equity. After a tough review, use it to clear your mental fog: reflect on the learnings, make it less personal, and give structure to your thoughts.
7. **Personalize:** AI can generate a lot of polished content, but it'll be robotic. Whoever reads what you share should have a sense that you wrote it. Give your agent five or six artifacts you wrote by hand as context for your writing style. Don't pass on AI slop. It might increase your productivity, but it's going to decrease your human intelligence.
8. **Be aware of your adeptness:** I think of four levels: awareness, exploration, enthusiasm, and AI native. Most rooms are much closer to the aware and explorer boundary. Being AI native means that whenever you pick up a non-human task, you think of AI first, instead of retrofitting it into the workflow.

## Pushing AI on harder problems

9. **Use AI for change management:** Humans are super bad at cascading a change through to what it means, and those blind spots surface later as surprises. Feed your status reports into a simple agent, and ask it not only what changed but what it means for you and your program.
10. **Use research agents to build KPIs:** There's no better way to resolve differences in opinion than upleveling the conversation to something measurable. We recently spent a month in ambiguity on how to measure a critical goal. Once the KPI was established, the conversations became so much easier.
11. **Automate a segment:** Automate a portion of a segment end to end: status updates, action item trackers, ticket creation, project retrospectives. That's when you get into a flywheel of automating more things.
12. **Use AI to be more inclusive:** You earn a lot of trust when you bring somebody into the fold who hasn't been able to make their voice heard. Use meeting summaries and comment patterns to see who has been contributing and who hasn't.

Supercharge your AI usage, but don't leave human intelligence behind. It's not an either-or: use the strengths of one to feed into the other.

## Q&A

**AI's answers on risk feel too prescriptive. Where has it helped you?**

Leave space. If you hand it your options, it just picks from choices already limited by your blind spots. I use it for change processing instead, and ask, "What am I not thinking about?"

**How do great TPMs deal with ambiguity?**

My org builds up-the-stack products on top of AWS building blocks like storage and compute, so most of our ambiguity comes from dependencies. Great TPMs know each dependency's pattern of behavior: which promises it keeps, and who else is asking it for the same thing. That gives a truer picture of what will land and what's at risk, and it's the unique value they create for me. It takes time to build, but AI can automate a lot of it once you have it. And great TPMs are comfortable with ambiguity but uncomfortable letting it linger.

**How do I put my own thinking first, instead of reaching for AI?**

Set the ground rules and the structure first. I can see a document in my mind before I write it. Once that's clear, engage AI. Don't wait on having a first version.
