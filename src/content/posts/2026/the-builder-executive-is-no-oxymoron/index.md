The vast majority of engineering executives that I find competent started their journey as builders. That building experience made them effective in their executive role. They could relate to the builders in their organization, stay close to the ground details, and use that as a signal to prioritize well. Their own experience as a grounding force had been effective for a long time, because the craft of building had only evolved linearly. But that grounding is no longer relevant, given the complete upheaval in the process of how builders build today. Today’s engineering executives need to have lived through what agents have done to building and coordination, immersively as a builder, to have conviction and credibility. They need to go through the experience of being a builder, while still being an executive. The opportunity cost should not be a concern when you truly grasp the scale of the transformation. AI is a transformative change on three fronts: a/ as a technology that enables new things, b/ as a new mechanism for how builders build, and c/ as a new paradigm for how teams operate. These are different, albeit related, dimensions.

## AI, as a technology

Intelligence is a building block, like compute and storage before it. It gets better and cheaper, and it can read, write, reason, and act, which wasn't possible a few years ago. The technology part of disruption is something executives have practice with. They learn how it gets built, talk to customers about how they use it, and read to keep up with the latest. Most of that applies here too. Learning the primitives, like transformers, attention, diffusion, and the layers of an AI stack, from the agent to inference to model training, and how they interact, will carry you a long way. But you will still fall very short if you stop here, because the bigger change is in how builders build and what teams look like now.

## AI, as a new way to build

Automated code generation is one very successful application of AI. It has driven the cost of software creation close to zero. To translate this into increased velocity, now you need to solve for the next biggest long poles which sit before and after the act of software creation. Before, so that the spec that the software is created for is of high quality, and that the task can be picked up and understood quickly. After, so that the audit, verification, and deployment are complete, fast, and safe. These are often areas that have long been afterthoughts at worst, and at best areas that struggled to get prioritized. Outdated documentation and context that make onboarding hard for engineers, and manual intervention required from engineering in verification and deployment have been common pain points for so many teams. Onboarding before, uninterrupted software generation in the middle, and verification after: that’s where the game is at now.

a/ *Specify and onboard:* Every piece of work starts with direction: a clear spec, and context an agent can pick up quickly. That makes onboarding the investment, because every agent that starts up is a new team member[^yanacek]. Skills, handoff notes, and context have to be written down once instead of explained every session. The spec has to be thought through so that software creation doesn't need babysitting. An agent that stops every few minutes with a small question still consumes the builder who answers it. Kiro's guide to frontier engineering puts the goal plainly: remove yourself from the loop until your involvement is limited to setting direction and verifying outcomes[^frontier]. To understand what you can achieve with this, you need to have lived through it immersively, and going through the most recent SDE bootcamp once won't teach you the nuances. When I started pushing myself on it, my coding tasks went from tens of minutes to a few hours without needing me. I was really happy the day an agent ran for 12 hours on an ambiguous training exercise.

b/ *Audit and verify:* With PRs multiplying fast, the judgment that matters most is which changes require builder review. Some don't. That means setting criteria: the critical parts of your codebase always get a builder, and small changes can go out with an agent's review alone. Teams are starting to write these criteria down: under a size limit, no serious findings from the review agent, an author who knows that code, with critical paths identified and carved out separately[^larson]. Agentic reviews also need careful design and mechanisms. It’s easy to have reviews stuck on pedantic comments and iterations that spiral and go nowhere. A spec that needs revisiting is a defect. A verification criterion that lets things slip through is a defect. Beyond increasing the velocity of ongoing changes, teams are also taking this opportunity to use AI to formally verify the correctness of critical pieces of their existing codebase against their specs[^formal].

c/ *Design the loops:* Builders are no longer the only triggers. A schedule, an alert, a security finding, or a drop in a KPI can start the work without anyone opening a ticket, and no builder directs or reviews each change. Once agents start and finish work on their own, the job becomes designing, tending to, and iteratively perfecting the loops: what they may change, how they check themselves, what they may spend, how they ensure the right governance, and when they stop. Creation of these loops is when agents start acting more like team members, rather than just tools that assist the builders. That upends the traditional thinking on how builder teams are designed, budgeted, and managed.

## AI, as a new team paradigm

The way we evaluate builders and run engineering teams has radically changed, starting with how work is resourced.

a/ *Budget and performance:* Historically, engineering executives focused on headcount (HC) as the primary currency to increase their impact. HC was a fixed expense, planned annually, with predictable upfront approval guardrails. In an agent-first paradigm, HC discussions lose their central place. Spend shifts from predictable fixed labor to highly variable API compute. Poorly constrained autonomous agents can consume a surprising amount of budget overnight without delivering proportional software value.

Performance management serves as the bridge between these two models. Just as you once managed human performance to protect your investment in headcount, you now manage agent performance to govern your variable compute spend. That requires setting strict evaluation criteria, bounding context limits, and preventing agent drift in continuous execution loops. Ultimately, this discipline extends back to the human builder: poor specs and lax verification steps are expensive mistakes that dilute your outcome per spend.

b/ *Complementary strengths:* You need to develop a new vocabulary for the inherent strengths of agents and models, one that guides what you turn to when solving a problem, the same way you pick or recruit builders for a problem based on what each does best. On my own team, Gemini is my inbound PM because it has the best knowledge of today’s world, Claude processes what comes in, Claude Code executes, ChatGPT is my outbound PM because it crafts words better, Muse is my procurement agent, and Gemma, which lives on my Mac, is my intern. Your own and your team’s roster might look different. The same vocabulary works a layer down, at the models themselves. When all you need is a decision, a decision model like TypeSafe AI's Jev is enough, and you don't need an LLM[^jev]. You need a similar understanding of the trade-offs in reasoning, latency, and context requirements per task and loop to pick the right agents and models.

c/ *Adversarial strengths:* Executives know how to maintain trust between builders. With humans, trust is earned slowly, memories can't be reset, and setting colleagues against each other has a cost. With agents, trust is granted task by task, and it's verified rather than assumed. You can scrub their memory and get a view that isn't anchored to earlier decisions, so the forgetting that makes onboarding a daily cost turns into a benefit. And you can use them as adversaries on purpose to tease out the truth: one agent attacks the plan another wrote, and a fresh session pokes holes in a design it has never seen. The agent that did the work shouldn't audit it. Greptile makes the case that the reviewer should come from a different model family than the author, because the two share blind spots, and that switching catches about 10% more bugs[^greptile]. With every new major frontier model release, I ask the agent to rigorously audit the design from the previous model per the spec.

Engineering executives who don't build will lose calibration fast, and they will miss in both directions. At times you will oversimplify with “put more agents on it”, and at times you will over-index on caution with “all code still needs final builder reviews”. Either way, you lose your team. Surface-level building creates a false sense of fluency, and it often hurts more than it helps. Real building requires more than desire. It requires commitment. How you execute on that commitment will be specific to your scenario. Practical mechanisms include joining your team’s spec and verification sessions purely as an observer, picking up a non-time-sensitive task from your team’s plate, and taking a week-long build-cation, say, every quarter. As they say, there is no compression algorithm for experience. AI reset what kind of experience counts for building, and the only way to re-earn your credentials is to build.
